How often does a model give a harmful answer?

A model that usually refuses a harmful request will, when sampled, sometimes comply. The rate is hard to measure: if it is one in ten thousand, plain sampling needs tens of thousands of answers to see it at all.

Angell et al. (ICML 2026, arXiv:2604.22167) sample instead from a copy of the model whose refusal direction has been ablated, which complies far more often, and weight each sample by the ratio of its probability under the original model to its probability under the ablated one. The weighted average estimates the original model's rate. On Banyan the ablation is a @steer step and the mixture of the two models a @sampling step; both probabilities of every token are stored on the tree, so the weight of a sample is one subtraction.

Here both sides run the same method on the same request: the authors' own code and Banyan, the same proposal, 1,000 answers each, the same judge.

Setup

How this was measured

the authors' code
Their estimator (smc/est_unsafe_to_unsafe.py, smc/estimator.py) unchanged, with three changes around it: the ablation hooks are built for every row (as shipped they are built for an empty slice of rows, so nothing is ablated; their paraphrase script and the paper ablate every row), the chat template's date is fixed to the day of their brute-force runs, and the judge is the same service Banyan uses. Their step loop notes the time at the start of every step, their judge call when it ends.
Banyan
The algorithm is our application on the user API. The measurement notes the time each node of the tree was made (the runtime's own new-node callback, no change to the system), and its @judge notes the time after each verdict.
the cost of noting
Each side ran the timed part twice, with and without the notes: the authors' code ; Banyan . The replay uses the runs with notes. Over three runs the authors' code took 34.3 to 35.1 s and Banyan 12.8 to 13.5 s.
