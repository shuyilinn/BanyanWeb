How often does a model give a harmful answer?

A model that usually refuses a harmful request will, when sampled, sometimes comply. The rate is hard to measure: if it is one in ten thousand, plain sampling needs tens of thousands of answers to see it at all.

Angell et al. (ICML 2026, arXiv:2604.22167) sample instead from a copy of the model whose refusal direction has been ablated, which complies far more often, and weight each sample by the ratio of its probability under the original model to its probability under the ablated one. The weighted average estimates the original model's rate.

On Banyan the ablation is a @steer step and the mixture of the two models a @sampling step. Both probabilities of every token are stored on the tree, so the weight of a sample is one subtraction.

Reading the picture

A dot is a node of the stored tree, a line joins a node to its parent, and the distance from the centre is the position of the node's token in the output. Both panels use one layout: the same sequence of tokens is at the same place in both, so where the two trees contain the same path they coincide. A tree too large to draw whole is drawn as the full paths from the root to a random sample of its nodes (drawn here: ). The slider moves through the order in which a run created its nodes, for the runs that recorded it; black dots were just created. The numbers under each panel are that run's measured totals.

Both panels are 1,000 answers to the same request, and both runs are Banyan's: there is no engine baseline for this method, so the left panel is the same model sampled without steering. A green dot is an answer the judge scores harmful. The dashed ring is the 150-token limit.

For this request plain sampling found 2 harmful answers in 1,000 and the steered proposal 13.

All six requests

Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct with the StrongREJECT requests and judge; 1,000 samples of up to 150 tokens per request on each side. Brute force is the authors' 10,000 plain samples per request. Banyan's estimates are below the brute-force rates: this version draws each token from the model's 512 most likely candidates and loses the original model's probability outside that set.
